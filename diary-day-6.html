✳ THE DEVELOPMENT DIARY / DAY 6

Teaching the house where things are.

Five receivers, a small beacon, and a tour of the kitchen. ♡

A room-by-room Bluetooth survey revealed a problem with our assumptions, and gave the AI engineering agent its next useful job.

Up to this point we'd done an awful lot of work to make the devices talk to one another, keep their data, and show the results in a proper application.

But the big idea hadn't changed. I wanted to put a tiny Bluetooth beacon on Mum's trolley and get a reasonable estimate of which room it was in, without fitting cameras all over her house or making her carry another gadget.

Before HomePulse can do that, it needs to learn what different locations actually look like to our Bluetooth receivers.

And that meant a bit of wandering around my own house.

First, give everything a name

We've now got a device-setup section in the private application. I could give devices understandable names and assignments instead of having to deal with manufacturer IDs or edit engineering files.

I put the five little Atom receivers around my house and labelled them. These were temporary positions for testing, not a claim that any receiver was already installed in its final location at Mum's.

The application also let me work with separate test sessions and named locations. That's important because when I eventually take this equipment to Mum's, we'll need a clean new set of readings from her house, not old observations from mine.

I don't want a demonstration in my kitchen to turn into a fictional record of Mum being in her kitchen.

Then I went for a walk

The actual recording process was delightfully simple.

I moved the beacon to a known position, picked the room in the application and gave it a more specific label. Something like Kitchen: Table or Kitchen: Sink.

Then I started the capture and waited while HomePulse gathered the Bluetooth observations, before moving to another place and doing it again.

From my point of view it was just a short wait, around 25 seconds at a time, then on to the next location. Behind the scenes the capture workflow keeps a window of data from before and after the trigger, along with the device identities, timestamps and signal quality.

That's a lot of engineering to support something as ordinary as walking from room to room and tapping Save.

And that simplicity is exactly what I wanted. Eventually, when I'm setting the system up at Mum's, I want to be able to do the physical part quickly, without carrying a laptop around and typing commands into it.

Except the sink wasn't playing nicely

Of course, real houses are rather less tidy than architectural diagrams.

One capture near the kitchen table came back with readings from all five receivers, marked Ready for review.

But at the kitchen sink, only four of the five were considered usable. An earlier attempt had been rejected, and a later one was still marked Incomplete capture.

At first glance that looks like a bad result. But it was telling us something genuinely useful.

Further testing showed that a receiver could be working perfectly well yet have a very weak or intermittent view of the beacon at particular positions. Walls, distance and the wireless environment all affect what a receiver can hear.

At another kitchen position, the bench readings showed four receivers with useful observations while a fifth had seen the beacon before the capture but not during the later part of the window. Other test locations had all five reporting.

So we'd found a problem, but not necessarily a broken receiver.

A rule that was too strict

When we looked into it, the calibration workflow had an assumption that sounded sensible when it was first written: to accept a location measurement, all five receivers should have usable beacon readings.

The real-world experiment exposed the flaw.

Imagine a receiver positioned near the garden. Should it have to hear the beacon when the trolley is at the far end of the house? Of course not. And a receiver that is healthy but too far away to hear the beacon is a different problem from one that's lost power or gone offline.

Those differences could actually help us identify locations, provided we record them honestly rather than inventing a very weak signal where none was observed.

So instead of moving receivers around just to satisfy the software, I've raised another engineering issue to tackle the problem.

The agent now has a job to investigate weak signals, intermittent observations, genuine non-detection and receivers that are unavailable altogether, and to make sure HomePulse can tell those situations apart.

It also has to think ahead. If one receiver goes offline after we've installed the system, HomePulse should still be able to make a cautious estimate from the remaining trustworthy readings when the evidence supports it, instead of assuming the entire house has gone quiet. If the readings can't distinguish two places, it should say the location is uncertain.

And the doorbell, button and other activity collection should carry on regardless.

WHAT DAY 6 TAUGHT US The calibration workflow works. The all-five rule doesn't fit every room.

I could label devices and test locations, capture real Bluetooth data and review the results. A kitchen-table capture had 5 of 5 receivers usable; a kitchen-sink capture had 4 of 5 and was correctly shown as incomplete under the current rules.

The new handling for partial, weak and missing readings is under development. Capturing and reviewing fingerprints is not the same as having a tested room-location classifier.

And that's where I'm leaving it tonight

I've given the agent the new issue and left it working while I head to bed.

I'm hoping that when I look again tomorrow, I'll be able to save sensible measurements from places like the kitchen sink without pretending every receiver must hear the beacon all the time.

But that's the next test, not today's result. The fix still needs implementation, review and a proper test suite before we can say it works.

There's something quite satisfying about this sort of discovery. We weren't sitting around inventing theoretical edge cases. I walked around my house using the application exactly as it was intended to be used, and reality challenged one of our engineering assumptions.

So now we improve the system.

And because I'm doing it with an agentic workflow, I can hand over a well-described problem, leave the investigation and implementation to the agent, and come back to review its evidence.

One more little step towards a HomePulse that fits Mum's real house, instead of expecting her house to fit our software.
