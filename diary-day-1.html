✳ THE DEVELOPMENT DIARY / DAY 1

The Bluetooth beacon.

How do you keep an eye on someone without watching them? ♡

A spare Raspberry Pi, five tiny receivers, an AI engineering agent and one very important rule: Mum's independence comes first.

There's something about Mum that hasn't changed, even though her mobility has deteriorated enormously over the last year.

Her determination to remain independent.

A year ago, she was playing football with my little boy. Now, getting from one room to another can be incredibly difficult.

When I visit to help her shower, she'll still try to stand up and do things herself, even when she's in pain.

“I'm perfectly capable of doing this on my own.”

And she means it. I sometimes remind her that it's okay to accept a little help while I'm there, because for the rest of the week she's having to manage those things herself.

I want her to keep as much independence as she safely can. But when I'm not there, I'd also like a little reassurance that things are carrying on as normal.

How do you keep an eye on someone without watching them?

We considered cameras around the house. Technically, that would be straightforward, but it felt far too intrusive, both for Mum and for anybody visiting her.

We looked at radar sensors, too. They could detect presence, but would give us more sensing than we really wanted without necessarily answering the question of which room Mum might be in.

PIR motion sensors were another option, but they'd mean installing more hardware in lots of places.

Then we came back to something Mum already uses: her walking trolley.

She relies on it to move between rooms. What if I put a tiny Bluetooth beacon on the trolley, and arranged a few small receivers around the house to listen for it?

Each receiver would report how strongly it could hear the beacon. By comparing those readings with a map of the house and, eventually, measurements taken in known locations, we could estimate which room the trolley was in.

It wouldn't be GPS or precise triangulation. Bluetooth signals reflect and fluctuate, particularly indoors. But a rough, confidence-labelled room estimate could still be useful.

The trolley isn't Mum

That's an important distinction. The beacon would tell us where the trolley was likely to be, not where Mum definitely was.

But because she depends so heavily on that trolley, and doesn't have another walker to use, its position could be a particularly useful clue.

Unless aliens come along and move it, of course. In which case I suspect we'll have bigger problems.

Still, a trolley can be moved by somebody else, left behind or misread by a receiver. Nothing about its position can prove Mum is safe or well.

The first thing I wanted to build was a simple picture of the house, with a marker showing the trolley's most likely room and how confident the system was. No complicated dashboard. Just useful context.

So how do I build it?

I'm a software engineer and technical solutions architect. I spend a lot of time working out how systems ought to behave, how their components fit together, and which engineering decisions will make them dependable.

I already understood the shape of this one: low-power Bluetooth receivers, a little computer to collect the readings, messaging between the devices and a local database.

But I didn't need to type every command or write every line of code myself.

The reason I use AI is time

There's another reason for using AI agents, and it's far more personal than wanting to try some clever technology.

I have a busy job as a technical solutions architect, working on some very large systems. I have a family, and I want to be there for my little boy and all his activities. I go skating and surfing, too. And I'm Mum's sole carer.

There simply aren't enough hours in the day to do all those things and also spend every spare evening writing a home-monitoring system from scratch.

It's not that I can't do the engineering myself. I can. The point is that I don't need to do every part of it by hand.

And there's a harder truth behind that decision. The longer I take to build something useful, the longer Mum goes without the benefit of it.

I don't want to look back and think I could have made her life a little easier sooner, but didn't because I refused to use AI on principle. That would be putting my feelings about a technology ahead of what she actually needs.

Of course, speed isn't much use if what I build is unreliable. The architecture still needs thought, the work still needs review, and the devices still need real testing. But if I can use agents responsibly to do more of the implementation, I can spend my time on the decisions, the checks, my family and, most importantly, Mum.

That's why I'm embracing agentic development. Not because engineering matters less, but because Mum matters more.

I've built an agentic engineering framework called HexJack. It allows me to organise and supervise AI engineering agents as part of a structured development process. I use Jira tickets for the work, review the decisions, and remain accountable for what gets built.

First, I gave a research agent the problem and had it investigate hardware options. I assessed its recommendations, challenged decisions where I thought it necessary, and made the hardware choices myself.

That led to a shopping list including five M5Stack AtomS3 Lite receivers, a SenseCAP BC03 beacon, and other HomePulse equipment for later stages.

Then I organised the build into Jira work items and started an engineering agent.

“Please insert the SD card.”

My initial gateway was an old Raspberry Pi 3 B-series computer I already had.

I told the agent what needed setting up, including Raspberry Pi OS, local storage, the messaging service and the Bluetooth receiver software. I gave it one particularly useful instruction: tell me whenever you need me to do something physical.

It asked for an SD card. I inserted one into the computer, then left it to prepare the operating system image.

Later, a notification arrived on my phone through NTFY. The agent needed me again.

It was time to take the SD card out, put it in the Pi, connect power and Ethernet, and let it boot.

I did those things, told the agent the Pi was ready, and it carried on with the configuration and checks.

That was the pattern for the afternoon. The agent handled what it could do in software. I acted as its hands whenever something needed plugging in, moving or restarting.

Five receivers and one beacon

When the agent asked for the Bluetooth receivers, I connected the five little units to the computer so it could configure their firmware, networking and messaging.

We also had to deal with an unreliable temporary Wi-Fi arrangement. There was some swapping and reconfiguration before the setup behaved well enough to continue.

Once the receivers were running, I moved them to ordinary USB power sockets, unplugged and reconnected devices, and followed the agent's instructions for testing whether they recovered.

Meanwhile the software was collecting beacon signal-strength reports through MQTT, the messaging system, and writing accepted readings into SQLite on the Raspberry Pi.

WHAT THE BENCH TEST PROVED Five receivers. One beacon. Real data.

In a recorded test on 19 November 2026, all five receivers contributed 3,814 signal reports over a little more than ten minutes. The data was stored in SQLite and the database integrity check passed.

One receiver had intermittent Wi-Fi trouble. So this was a successful bench test, not proof of reliable room detection in Mum's house.

And then there was a pull request

By the end of the first day, we'd prepared the Pi, configured all five Bluetooth receivers, captured real signal readings, and tested important aspects of recovery when devices or the Pi restarted.

We weren't identifying rooms yet. There was still calibration to do, real-house testing to plan, and plenty of reliability work ahead. But the foundation was there.

The agent's work went through the usual engineering process, including a pull request and my review.

Later, I was telling a friend how little direct interaction the whole process had needed. He seemed genuinely impressed, and we ended up talking about the wider possibilities of AI working with physical hardware.

That conversation made me appreciate the change in how I work. I hadn't handed an AI an idea and hoped for the best. I'd designed an approach, organised the work, challenged the research, supervised the process and reviewed the result.

The agent did the work it could do. I did the work that required a human.

One afternoon down. A working Bluetooth foundation. And a much bigger experiment starting to take shape.

Not a safety monitor

HomePulse is a personal home-awareness experiment, not a medical device or emergency-response service. Bluetooth reports and room estimates are imperfect clues, not proof of a person's location, wellbeing or safety. Direct human contact remains essential when there's cause for concern.
