✳ THE DEVELOPMENT DIARY / DAY 3

Giving the Pi a way home.

Because I can't be at Mum's every time a gadget needs attention. ♡

Our Raspberry Pi could collect the clues. The next question was how I could look after it once it was living somewhere else.

We've made rather a lot of progress in a short space of time.

First, we got five little Bluetooth receivers listening for the beacon on Mum's trolley. Then we connected the doorbell, camera, smart-home hub, button and door contacts to the same Raspberry Pi, so their events could appear in one timeline.

But there's a problem with building something for Mum's house when the development equipment is still sitting at mine.

What happens when I install it there and something needs fixing?

I can't reasonably pop over every time a receiver loses its connection, a setting needs checking or the little computer needs attention. And I certainly don't want Mum having to become the household IT department.

She's got quite enough to do.

Make it maintainable before taking it to Mum's

The sensible answer is to give the Pi a secure way to contact a service that I can use to administer it remotely.

That sounds rather ordinary, but the details matter. I don't want to open Mum's home network to the internet or ask her to change router settings. I don't even know what her final network arrangement will look like until we commission the system.

So the plan was for the Pi to make an outbound, encrypted connection through AWS Systems Manager. With the right authorisation, I could then open a restricted maintenance session without exposing a public login port.

It's the digital equivalent of being able to inspect the equipment without driving over and finding somewhere to plug a laptop in.

It also fits the original HomePulse principle: the technology should adapt to Mum's life, not the other way around.

Of course, the Pi had other ideas

This is where the engineering became a little less straightforward.

The Pi is an older, perfectly serviceable machine, and a recent version of the AWS software wasn't happy with its processor. The first compatible-looking ARM64 components failed because they expected an instruction the Pi simply doesn't support.

It would have been easy to mistake that for a broken setup and start changing things at random.

Instead, the investigation identified the compatibility problem and found a signed, supported packaging route that actually ran on this machine.

That might not sound very glamorous, but it's exactly the sort of thing I want from an engineering process: diagnose the real problem, make a considered change, then test the result.

AI agents can undertake a lot of that investigation and implementation, but they still need sound architecture, constraints, reviews and real evidence.

♡

Getting it working is one thing.
Being able to look after it is another.

Could we actually reach it?

We didn't stop at installing a service and assuming everything was fine.

On 23 November, the Raspberry Pi registered with AWS and showed as online. An authorised operator session connected to it, identified the machine and then closed normally.

Even better, the remote session had deliberately limited permissions. It wasn't a general-purpose, unrestricted way into the computer. The operator account could inspect what it needed, without simply being handed full administrative control.

We also checked that the existing HomePulse services were still healthy. Our Bluetooth collection, messaging and Tapo event collectors hadn't been sacrificed to make the remote connection work.

That last check matters. I don't want the maintenance system breaking the very thing it's supposed to help maintain.

And what about the little USB receivers?

There's another reason I was particularly interested in doing this before installation.

When we move HomePulse to Mum's, the five Bluetooth receivers will need to connect to her Wi-Fi rather than the temporary network we've been using for testing.

The receivers are small, USB-powered devices. In principle, being able to connect one directly to the Raspberry Pi gives us a handy way to communicate with it without having to return it to my computer.

So we took a receiver that was physically plugged into the Pi and tried something simple through the remote maintenance session.

Could the Pi identify it and tell us what software it was running?

Yes. The session queried the receiver over USB, and it replied with its identity and firmware version.

That's a genuine, useful result. It proves the restricted remote connection can reach the Pi and perform a read-only conversation with the attached device.

It doesn't yet prove that we can safely change a receiver's Wi-Fi credentials remotely. That needs a separate test before I'd rely on it during installation. But we've removed one important unknown.

WHAT DAY 3 PROVED Remote maintenance works on the home bench.

The Pi was reachable through an authorised AWS session, with no new public inbound ports. A receiver connected to the Pi's USB port returned a valid identity and firmware response. The existing Bluetooth and Tapo collection services remained healthy.

This is engineering access, not a finished family dashboard or an installed system at Mum's.

All this work to save a journey?

In a way, yes. But that's rather the point.

I have a demanding job architecting large systems, a family, a little boy with his own busy schedule, and things I enjoy doing myself. I'm also Mum's sole carer.

Every unnecessary journey or afternoon spent troubleshooting something that could have been fixed remotely takes time away from somewhere else.

I'm not using agentic development because I can't do the engineering. I'm using it because I can make the architectural decisions, direct the work and check the results without having to perform every routine step myself.

That doesn't mean rushing untested equipment into Mum's house. It means doing the hard thinking and testing now, so that once it's there, it asks as little of her as possible.

And if I can solve a technical problem from home rather than asking Mum to fiddle with a USB cable, that's a win for both of us.

So, can I see it on my phone now?

Not quite. The secure engineering connection is working, but it's not the everyday screen I actually want to use.

The next stage is a simple, properly designed HomePulse dashboard: recent events, how fresh the information is, whether the receivers are online, and what the system can and can't tell us.

After that, we need to make that view privately accessible from my phone or laptop, without turning Mum's network into a public website.

And later, once the equipment is installed and we've surveyed the real rooms, we can work on the original ambition: a rough, honest estimate of where her trolley is.

For now, though, we've achieved something that isn't particularly exciting to look at but makes the whole project more practical.

The Pi can collect the clues. And now, when it needs attention, I have a way to reach it.
